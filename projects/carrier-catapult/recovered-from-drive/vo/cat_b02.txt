Has anyone made it work? Two navies have. And their stories run in opposite directions.

Let's start with China. China never sent a steam catapult to sea. From twenty sixteen, it was throwing a test jet from catapults built on land. Nine years later, in November twenty twenty-five, it commissioned the carrier Fujian, with electric catapults.

Fujian has already thrown a flying radar plane into the air. And she is not nuclear powered. So you do not need a reactor for this machine. You need stored power.

And America, which invented it? Its first ship with electric catapults is the U-S-S Ford. The American Navy wanted more than four thousand launches between serious failures.

In twenty twenty, the testers measured one hundred and eighty-one. In twenty twenty-two, six hundred and fourteen. Far short.

But the ship did not stop flying. By the programme's own count, the Ford has made more than thirty-six thousand launches. Steam catapults had many decades to mature. This one is still young.

Even so, this August, the American President ordered a plan to go back to steam on a future carrier. The company that builds the electric one has asked the government to think again. As this film is made, nothing final has been announced.

So the inventor is in two minds, and the newcomer is already flying. Where does that leave India?

In Pune, in Maharashtra, engineers at a D-R-D-O laboratory are building India's own electric catapult.

Remember the lorry? This is it. At the Bengaluru air show last year, it stood on a trailer. As reported, it is meant to throw up to four hundred kilos, over about seventeen metres.

I say as reported, because D-R-D-O itself has published very little about it.

Now put the Navy's need beside it, and you see the gap. Not four hundred kilos. Thirty tonnes. Not seventeen metres. About a hundred. That is seventy-five times the weight.

A full-size one, say officials quoted in the defence press, is a decade or more away.

You might ask me, Kishore, who is stopping India from building its own? Nobody. Nobody is stopping us.

But look at what China had, that India does not have yet. A full-size test track on land. And a ship waiting for the machine.

The Navy's own fifteen-year plan asks for two electric catapults. But the next carrier close to approval, at an estimated forty thousand crore rupees, is a repeat of Vikrant. With a ramp.

Then why not just buy one? You may remember the reports in twenty seventeen, that America was ready to sell it to India. Nothing was signed. Later, America cleared the same system for France. The estimate for one ship's set, with the wires that stop a landing jet, was one point three billion dollars. Around eleven thousand crore rupees, before you build the ship.

So let me put it to you plainly. The jets are ordered. Twenty-six Rafales, for about sixty-three thousand crore rupees. The catapult is the size of a lorry. And the ship is not approved. Until that changes, India's Rafales will fly from ramps.

Now look at what India does have. This is the Navy that took a catapult to war on a cracked boiler. And India designed and built the new Vikrant itself.

What is missing is a decision, a test track, and ten patient years.

And you should hear the other side too. Some of India's own defence leaders have said, submarines and the Army must come first. That argument is one reason the ship still waits.

So what should you carry home? The motor is a cousin of your ceiling fan. The energy is six days of your home. Everything hard is in the three seconds.

You can buy the jet. You have to build the throw.

Three things for you to watch. The decision on the next carrier. The first Indian Rafale, due around twenty twenty-nine. And the day that lorry in Pune throws something heavier.

If this made a hard subject simple for you, subscribe. When that decision comes, you and I will open up that deck together.

And tell me in the comments. If that forty thousand crore were your money, what would you build — a ramp, or a catapult?
