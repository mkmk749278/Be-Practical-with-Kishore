## The narration (as it will be voiced; `vo.txt` is the recording text)

In nineteen seventy four, Canada walked out of a half built nuclear reactor on the Chambal river, in Rajasthan.

India finished it. But twenty years later, its heart was wearing out. And who could you call for help?

Only one country had ever replaced a reactor's heart like this. Canada. And Canada's export rules kept its companies from helping.

So how do you rebuild the heart of a machine that nobody will teach you to open?

By the end, you will see Indian engineers do it alone, inside that same reactor. And you will see why it matters for the new reactor being loaded with fuel on the same lake, right now.

Let me take you to Rawatbhata. A small town on the Rana Pratap Sagar lake, about sixty four kilometres from Kota.

In the nineteen sixties, Canada and India built a nuclear power station here together. Its design came from Canada. Why that design? India had picked it for one simple reason.

It needs no enriched uranium. You can run it on natural uranium, with the same mix of atoms you find in nature. And Indian factories could build it.

Now, you have probably seen a reactor drawn as one giant steel pot. Most reactors in the world are just that.

This one is different. Imagine a tank, pierced by three hundred and six tubes. Each tube is its own small pressure cooker.

Think of the cooker in your kitchen. You know that whistle. Inside, the steam pushes at about one kilo on every square centimetre. In India's newest reactors, the water in each tube is pushed a hundred times harder, at three hundred and ten degrees.

And in reactors of this family, the wall of that tube is only about four millimetres thick. Hold up a pencil. The wall is thinner than that.

Then came May nineteen seventy four. Pokhran.

Why did Canada care so much? India called its test peaceful. Canada saw plutonium from a research reactor near Mumbai, called Sigh-rus, which Canada had helped design.

So, as you can guess, Canada stopped its nuclear help. The engineers packed up and went home. And what do you do with half a reactor?

The first unit was running. The second, as an Indian magazine put it in nineteen ninety six, the Canadians abandoned halfway.

You might ask me, Kishore, so what happened to your half built reactor? Indian engineers finished it on their own. It went to work in nineteen eighty one.

If you are watching this on the big T-V, pick up your phone and point the camera at the screen. Every Sunday, eleven thirty in the morning, we open one machine like this. Now, back to Rawatbhata.

Now here is a number that should surprise you. At Kaiga in Karnataka, a reactor of this family once ran for nine hundred and sixty two days without stopping. A world record.

So what stops a machine like that? You might guess the fuel.

It is not the fuel. Fresh fuel goes in while it runs. What wears out is the tubes.

The early tubes were made of an older alloy. Over the years they soaked up hydrogen. N-P-C-I-L says they lasted only ten to twelve years.

And what happens when a tube gives up? In August nineteen eighty three, near Toronto, the world saw.

At the Pickering station, one tube broke at full power. Just one, out of hundreds. But that is all it takes.

How does a steel tube just break? Each hot tube sits inside a cooler tube, held apart by small coiled springs, like rings on a finger.

In that one channel, a ring had slipped out of place. Slowly, the hot tube sagged. Then it touched the cold one.

Now think of a glass of cold water on a hot day. You have seen the drops gather on the outside, on the cold glass.

Inside the metal, hydrogen did the same thing you saw on that glass. It moved to the coldest spot. There it formed hard, brittle blisters.

The blisters cracked. And the crack kept growing, until the tube split.

The operators, you should know, brought the reactor safely to a stop. But every reactor of this kind now had a question to answer.

Now you can see the problem. India's tubes at Rawatbhata were made of the same older alloy. And as Business India reported, Indian engineers were denied detailed knowledge of the accident, its cause and the remedy.

So India had the same disease. And no doctor would come. What would you have done?

In nineteen ninety five, a trade weekly reported India's decision. Rajasthan unit two would get all new tubes, with or without outside help.

India was still part of a club of reactor owners. But Canada's export rules kept Canadian firms from supplying the tools, or doing the job.

So the job came home, to people you have never heard of.

The project director at Rawatbhata was V K Chaturvedi. The team that would change the tubes was led by R C Arya.

Can you guess the hardest part? Every one of the three hundred and six tubes had been inside a running reactor for years. You cannot stand near it. Not for a minute.

So every cut, every pull, every weld had to be done by machines. Machines that did not exist yet. Indian engineers designed them.

Now picture one old tube with me. A tool reaches in and cuts it free. The tube is drawn, slowly, into a heavy steel flask.

The flask carries it away to a vault. Nobody stands beside it. Everything is done from a distance.

Then, as you watch, a new tube goes in. This time, made of a newer alloy, zirconium with niobium. And then the next. Three hundred and six times.

And what did all this cost? Business India reported a plan of seventy two million dollars, against nearly three hundred million in Canada.

At that year's rate, that is about two hundred and fifty crore rupees here, against more than a thousand crore there.

Was it quick? No. The reactor made no power in nineteen ninety five, ninety six or ninety seven.

By nineteen ninety eight, Rajasthan unit two was running again.

And do you know what N-P-C-I-L calls it? The first such job in India, and only the second in the world.

Bhabha Atomic Research Centre later counted what tools like these gave back. About one thousand one hundred crore rupees of extra electricity.

You remember how our fathers never threw away a broken radio? They opened it. That is the lesson of Rawatbhata.

A machine is truly yours only when you can open it, and mend it.

But I must be fair with you. You will hear people say these tubes are dangerous. So let me show you the worst day.

The eleventh of March, twenty sixteen. Kakrapar, in Gujarat. At eight fifty two in the morning, a tube began to leak.

The reactor shut itself down, the way you would want it to. There was no fuel failure. The regulator reported no radiological impact on workers, the public or the environment.

How serious was it? The international scale rated it two, out of seven. An incident, not an accident.

I will tell you honestly, the cause took time to find. An impurity in the gas around the tubes. It had eaten small spots into their outer walls.

The regulator found it only in Kakrapar's two units.

So what would you do now? Kakrapar did what Rawatbhata had taught. Every tube came out. Three hundred and six in each reactor.

A former chief of N-P-C-I-L said it plainly. Replacing them is like a heart transplant for a human being.

Was it the end of the story? No. It was the start of a bigger one.

Now look at the reactor face with me. Three hundred and six circles grow to three hundred and ninety two.

This is India's own seven hundred megawatt reactor, designed here. And each tube is watched by a gas whose dew point tells the engineers if even a drop of water leaks.

Do you remember the glass of cold water? Here, the dew is the alarm.

The first one, at Kakrapar, went critical on the twenty second of July, twenty twenty, at nine thirty six in the morning. Its fuel had gone in that March. Many of its tests were done during the lockdown.

And Rawatbhata, where you began this story, got its own. Unit seven joined the grid in March twenty twenty five, and was running at full power by February twenty twenty six.

And this September, on the same lake where Canada walked out, unit eight began taking in its fuel. Four thousand seven hundred and four bundles, for three hundred and ninety two tubes.

Now the confession, and you deserve it. After all these years, nuclear gives India only about three per cent of its electricity. About one turn in thirty of your ceiling fan.

And were the new units on time? No. They were slow too. Kakrapar's first concrete was poured in twenty ten. It went critical ten years later.

You may have read that the government has approved ten more, as one fleet. It said about seventy thousand crore rupees of orders would go to Indian industry.

And here is the quiet ending to the old fight. Earlier this year, a Canadian company signed to sell India nearly twenty two million pounds of uranium, from twenty twenty seven to twenty thirty five.

Do you see it? Not revenge. Two countries, trading again.

Think about what that tells you. The fuel, India can buy. The skill to open its own reactor, nobody could sell. India had to grow it.

So here is your date to watch. The day Rajasthan unit eight goes critical. When it does, it will stand on the same ground Canada left.

Our fathers kept their radios for years. Not because they were stubborn. Because they could open them.

A machine is truly yours only when you can open it, and mend it.
