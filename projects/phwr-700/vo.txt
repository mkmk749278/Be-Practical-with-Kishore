In nineteen seventy four, Canada walked out of a half built nuclear reactor on the Chambal river, in Rajasthan.

India finished it. But twenty years later, its heart was wearing out. Three hundred and six thin metal tubes.

Only one country had ever replaced tubes like these. Canada. And Canada's export rules kept its companies from helping.

So how do you rebuild the heart of a machine that nobody will teach you to open?

By the end, you will see Indian engineers pull those tubes out, one by one, into a steel flask. And you will see why it matters for the new reactor that began taking in fuel this September, on the same lake.

Let me take you to Rawatbhata. A small town on the Rana Pratap Sagar lake, about sixty four kilometres from Kota.

If you had come here in the nineteen sixties, you would have seen Canada and India building a nuclear power station together. Its design came from Canada. And why that design? India had picked it for one simple reason.

It needs no enriched uranium. You can run it on natural uranium, with the same mix of atoms you find in nature. And Indian factories could build it.

Now, you have probably seen a reactor drawn as one giant steel pot. Most reactors in the world are just that.

This one is different. Imagine a tank, pierced by three hundred and six tubes. Each tube, if you like, is its own small pressure cooker.

Think of the cooker in your kitchen. You know that whistle, you have heard it all your life. Inside, the steam pushes at about one kilo on every square centimetre.

Now jump ahead to India's newest reactors for a moment. There, the water in each tube is pushed a hundred times harder, at three hundred and ten degrees.

And in reactors of this family, the wall of that tube is only about four millimetres thick. Think of a pencil. The wall is thinner than that.

Then came May nineteen seventy four. You remember it. Pokhran.

Why did Canada care so much? India called its test peaceful. Canada saw plutonium from a research reactor near Mumbai, called Sigh-rus, which Canada had helped design.

So Canada stopped its nuclear help. The engineers packed up and went home.

The first unit was running. The second, as an Indian magazine put it in nineteen ninety six, the Canadians abandoned halfway.

Now you might ask me, Kishore, so what happened to your half built reactor? Indian engineers finished it on their own. It went to work in nineteen eighty one.

And for a while, all was well. At Kaiga in Karnataka, a reactor of this family once ran for nine hundred and sixty two days without stopping. A world record.

So what stops a machine like that? You might guess the fuel.

It is not the fuel. You can feed fresh fuel in while it runs. What wears out is the tubes.

The early tubes were made of an older metal mix. Over the years they soaked up hydrogen. N-P-C-I-L, the company that runs India's nuclear plants, says they lasted only ten to twelve years.

In August nineteen eighty three, near Toronto, the world saw what that means.

At the Pickering station, one tube broke at full power. Just one, out of hundreds. But that is all it takes.

How does a tube just break? Each hot tube sits inside a cooler tube. Small coiled springs, wrapped around it, keep the two apart.

In that one channel, a spring had slipped out of place. Slowly, the hot tube sagged. Then it touched the cold one.

Now think of a glass of cold water on a hot day. You have seen the drops gather on the outside, on the cold glass.

Inside the metal, hydrogen did something like the drops you just pictured. It crept to the coldest spot. And there it hardened into brittle blisters.

The blisters cracked. And the crack kept growing, until the tube split.

The operators, I should tell you, brought the reactor safely to a stop. But every reactor of this kind now had a question to answer.

India's tubes at Rawatbhata were made of the same older metal. And as Business India reported, Indian engineers were denied detailed knowledge of the accident, its cause and the remedy.

So India had the same disease. And no doctor would come. What would you have done?

In nineteen ninety five, a nuclear trade weekly reported India's decision. Rajasthan unit two would get all new tubes, with or without outside help.

India was still a member of the Kan-doo Owners Group, the club of countries that run this kind of reactor. But Canada's export rules kept Canadian firms from supplying the tools, or doing the job.

So the job came home.

The project director at Rawatbhata was V K Chaturvedi. The team that would change the tubes was led by R C Arya.

Can you picture the hardest part? Every one of the three hundred and six tubes had been inside a running reactor for years. You cannot stand near it. Not for a minute.

So every cut, every pull, every weld had to be done by machines. Machines that did not exist yet. Indian engineers designed them.

Now picture one old tube with me. A tool reaches in and cuts it free. The tube is drawn, slowly, into a heavy steel flask.

The flask carries it away to a vault. You will not see a single person beside it. Everything is done from a distance.

Then, as you watch, a new tube goes in. This time, made of a stronger mix, zirconium with niobium. And then the next. Three hundred and six times.

And what did all this cost? Going by Business India's figures, turned into rupees at that year's rate, India planned to spend about two hundred and fifty crore.

Canada had spent more than a thousand crore on the same kind of job.

Was it quick? No. The reactor made no power in nineteen ninety five, ninety six or ninety seven.

By nineteen ninety eight, if you drove past Rawatbhata, you would have seen unit two running again.

N-P-C-I-L calls it the first such job in India, and only the second in the world. The first was in Canada itself.

Bhabha Atomic Research Centre later counted what tools like these gave back. About one thousand one hundred crore rupees of extra electricity.

But I must be fair with you. You may have heard people say these tubes are dangerous. Is it safe to live near one? Let me show you the worst day.

The eleventh of March, twenty sixteen. Kakrapar, in Gujarat. At about nine in the morning, a tube began to leak.

The reactor shut itself down, the way you would want it to. There was no fuel failure. The regulator said there was no radiation harm to the workers, to the people outside, or to the land around it.

So how serious was it? The international scale rated it two, out of seven. An incident, not an accident.

What went wrong? A wrong chemical had got into the gas around the tubes. It had eaten small spots into their outer walls.

And was it everywhere? No. The regulator found it only in Kakrapar's two units.

So, as you might expect, Kakrapar did what Rawatbhata had taught. Every tube came out. Three hundred and six in each reactor.

A former chief of N-P-C-I-L put it in words you will not forget. Replacing them is like a heart transplant for a human being.

And that was the start of a bigger story.

Now look at the reactor face with me, and count with your eyes. Three hundred and six circles grow to three hundred and ninety two.

This is India's own seven hundred megawatt reactor, designed here. Around each tube flows a dry gas. If even a drop of water leaks in, the gas turns damp, like the dew on your cold glass.

Remember that glass? Here, the dew is the alarm.

The first one, at Kakrapar, went critical in July twenty twenty. Critical? That is good news here. It means the chain reaction has started, and holds steady. Many of its tests were done during the lockdown.

And Rawatbhata, where this story began, got its own. Unit seven joined the grid in March twenty twenty five, and was running at full power by February twenty twenty six.

And this September, on the same lake you saw at the start, where Canada walked out, unit eight began taking in its fuel. About four thousand seven hundred bundles, for three hundred and ninety two tubes.

Now the honest part, which you deserve. After all these years, out of every hundred units of electricity India makes, only about three come from nuclear.

And were the new units on time? No. Kakrapar's first concrete was poured in twenty ten. It went critical ten years later.

Not revenge. Two countries, trading again.
