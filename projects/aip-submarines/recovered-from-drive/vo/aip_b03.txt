Then you might ask, why not just build nuclear submarines? India does build them. But they are far bigger, far more costly, and few. In the shallow waters close to our coast, a small, quiet diesel boat is still the right tool.

So India built its own A-I-P. And it built it in its own way.

Germany's fuel cell submarines carry their hydrogen locked inside metal tanks. India's carries no hydrogen gas at all.

Instead, it carries a dry white powder. Sodium boro-hydride.

Add water, and the powder gives off hydrogen. Right there, inside the submarine, only as much as you need.

How much power is hiding in that powder? One spoonful, about seven grams, makes enough electricity to charge your phone. That's our rough calculation, on paper. Now picture tonnes of it.

The hydrogen then meets oxygen. The oxygen is carried as a liquid, kept very, very cold. The two meet inside a fuel cell, and its heart is an acid. You won't see a flame. No explosion. Just a steady flow of electricity. And one more thing comes out. Water. That water flows back to the powder, to make more hydrogen.

Would you want all this inside a sealed steel tube with you? In October twenty twenty-six, India's Defence Secretary said it simply. These systems need a strict safety culture, with no compromise.
