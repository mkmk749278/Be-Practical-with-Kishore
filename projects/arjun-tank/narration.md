## The narration (exactly as it will be recorded)

Chennai, February twenty twenty-one. The Prime Minister hands India's newest tank, the Arjun Mark One A, to the Army.

Seven months later, India orders one hundred and eighteen of them. So where are they?

You would expect them in regiments by now. The first were due in twenty twenty-four. As of January this year, reports say, not one had been delivered. None has been reported since.

The design is ready. The order is signed. What is missing is the engine.

You might think India simply cannot make one. But in twenty twenty-four, in Mysuru, India's own tank engine roared for the first time.

So why are the tanks still waiting? You will see what that engine must do, why the old one stopped coming, and the two ways India can end the wait.

Think back to twenty twenty-one. Maybe you booked a new car that year. And the dealer said, waiting period. Eight months. Ten months. Why? A tiny chip was missing.

The Army booked its tanks that same year. On the twenty-third of September, the Ministry of Defence ordered one hundred and eighteen Arjuns from the Heavy Vehicles Factory in Avadi, Chennai.

About seven and a half thousand crore rupees. Roughly sixty-four crore for each tank, by my count. And work for more than two hundred Indian suppliers, the Ministry said.

The plan was simple. Five tanks within thirty months. Then thirty a year, until about twenty twenty-seven.

Then, in February twenty twenty-four, a D-R-D-O official told the defence journal Janes that the tanks would be late. The reason? Engines from a German company, M-T-U, were delayed.

Later that year, reports said the factory could offer about twelve tanks, using older spare engines. The Army was said to be unwilling. Twelve tanks would not fill even one squadron. Would you take twelve, and wait years for the rest?

You have read the comments under every Arjun video. Too heavy. Only good for the desert. German engine, so not really Indian. Hold on to those. I will answer each one in this film.

But first, the engine. Your car was missing a chip. This tank is missing its heart.

Now picture yourself at the back of the tank, with me beside you. Under the rear deck, hidden from you, is a steel room. In it sits the powerpack — the engine and the gearbox, bolted together as one block.

The engine is a German diesel, the M-T-U eight thirty-eight. One thousand four hundred horsepower. You know your Maruti Swift? Put about seventeen of your Swift engines in one box. That is this engine.

Sounds like a lot, doesn't it? But it has to move about sixty-eight tonnes. On tracks. Through sand. In the Thar.

So every tonne of this tank gets only about twenty horsepower. That is not a lot, for a machine that must climb dunes and fight.

Now you might ask, why not just buy another engine? From anyone?

Because a tank engine is not a part you swap. It is a room. Its size, its weight, its cooling, its air filters, the gearbox it bolts to — all of it is shaped to this one hull. And the gearbox, too, is German. A company called Renk.

Change the engine, and you must redesign the room. Then you test the whole tank again, from the start. That takes you years.

So the engine decides everything. Then why did India have to buy it from abroad? For that, we go back fifty-two years.

May, nineteen seventy-four. The government asks D-R-D-O to design India's own battle tank. The design lab is in Avadi. The factory that will build it is there too.

Thirty years later, around two thousand four, the first Arjuns join the Army. Two regiments, near Jaisalmer, in the Rajasthan desert.

India designed it, and India built it. You can be proud of that. But the government's own auditor later wrote that at induction, sixty-nine per cent of the tank's components were imported.

Now here is the sentence that hurts. The spare parts were not being made in India. So from twenty thirteen, the auditor wrote, the Arjun was not operational.

Think about that. Not destroyed. Not defeated. Just waiting for parts you could not buy.

So was the tank itself any good? In twenty ten, near Bikaner, the Army put fourteen Arjuns against fourteen T-ninety tanks. Its own report, which you can read in the auditor's files, found the Arjun marginally better in accuracy.

The T-ninety won on overall score, mostly on missile firing, which the Arjun was not built for. And the Army's verdict? The Arjun had performed creditably.

The pattern is clear. The body was good. What failed was what India had bought from outside.

Now, the comment you have read most, maybe even written yourself. It is too heavy.

Look at the numbers yourself. The newest Arjun weighs about sixty-eight tonnes. A T-ninety weighs under fifty. But do you know why the Arjun grew? After those trials, the Army itself asked for more — a missile, better shells, extra armour. Reports say that added about six tonnes.

So can it go wherever you need it? Honestly, no. The news website The Wire reported in twenty twenty-one that most bridges in Punjab were built for loads of about fifty tonnes. A former armoured corps general called it a tank only for a few pockets of the desert.

He has a point. But on open sand, the Army's own verdict stands. The Arjun can attack and defend as well as the T-ninety. And open sand, if you look at the map, is where its regiments live.

And if you are thinking of Ladakh, you are right. For the mountains, India is building something else. A light tank, called Zorawar. In December twenty twenty-four, it fired at more than four thousand two hundred metres.

And the T-ninety? India has more than a thousand. It is a Russian design, built under licence, in the same factory at Avadi. Different tanks, for different ground, as you would expect.

Here is the difference you should notice. The weight, India chose. The engine, India did not.

So let me take you to the other side. Friedrichshafen, on Lake Constance, in southern Germany. This is where M-T-U builds its engines. Today the company is part of Rolls-Royce.

If you run a factory like this, an engine model is a line of machines, tools, and trained hands. When the orders stop, the line stops. And this engine is an old model.

Reports say the company would not restart it for about one hundred and twenty engines. It did not pay. It offered a newer engine instead, which meant years of fresh trials.

Was that unfair to India? If you were running that factory, would you restart it? Probably not. A business made a business decision. The risk was always ours — depending on one foreign line for the heart of our tank.

And if you think this happens only to India, you should look at Turkey. Its new tank, Altay, was meant to have a German engine and gearbox. Germany restricted the export, over Turkey's operations in Syria. Turkey waited, and went to Korea.

Korea's own tank had the opposite trouble. Its home-made gearbox failed the endurance tests six times. So Korea bought a German one.

So even countries with big defence industries get stuck on the engine. You are not looking at an Indian failure alone.

Meanwhile, did you know that back in Avadi, India's own engine factory keeps the old Arjuns alive? In its last annual report, it says it repaired two of these German engines, and is learning to make the parts that wear out.

Then, in November twenty twenty-five, a twist you may have missed. Rolls-Royce reportedly said it was ready to make this same engine entirely in India, with full transfer of technology. It was waiting for the Ministry's clearance. None has been reported since.

So you might ask me, Kishore, is this the Tejas story again? India builds the machine, and waits for the engine?

Partly. But here is the part you have probably not heard.

Come with me to Mysuru, the twentieth of March, twenty twenty-four. At the engine division of B-E-M-L, with the Defence Secretary watching, India's first home-made tank engine is fired, officially, for the first time.

One thousand five hundred horsepower. A hundred more than the German one. Built for desert heat up to fifty-five degrees, and for the cold of the mountains.

The Defence Secretary called it ninety per cent Indian. The drawings and the design, he said, are all ours.

And it has a partner. A gearbox, also Indian, also one thousand five hundred horsepower, called Datran. Engine and gearbox together. An Indian heart.

On the fourth of December, twenty twenty-five, at Avadi, engineers began assembling a test vehicle to carry them.

But I must be fair with you, as I promised. The project was meant to finish by the middle of twenty twenty-five. It has not. And an engine that runs on a test bed is not yet an engine that drives sixty-eight tonnes across the Thar, year after year.

So when will you know it has worked? When that test vehicle passes its trials, and the Army's own. That proof is still to come. And it matters far beyond the Arjun. India's future tanks are meant to use this same engine. So the next generation of Indian tanks rides on these trials.

Now come back with me to that steel room at the back of the tank. You remember it. Empty, and waiting.

India has two ways to fill it. The old German engine, made here under licence, fits that room as it is. India's own engine means changing the room, and new trials. That is why it takes longer. But once it passes, it is ours for good.

If you ask me, the honest answer is both. Make the old engine here, to fill these one hundred and eighteen tanks. And keep proving our own, for every tank after them. That way, you get tanks sooner, and an engine of your own later.

So what should you watch for? Two things. The test vehicle's trials at Avadi. And the Ministry's answer on the Rolls-Royce offer.

Because armour, you can build. The heartbeat has to be yours.

If this film helped you see what is really holding back the Arjun, subscribe. When those trials report, you and I will open that steel room again.

And tell me in the comments. Would you wait for India's own engine, or take the German one, made here, to get the tanks sooner?

This is Be Practical with Kishore. Stay practical.
